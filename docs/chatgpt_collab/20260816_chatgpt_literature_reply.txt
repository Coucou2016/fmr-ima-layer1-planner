Below is the manuscript strategy I would use. The central positioning should be: this paper does not re-run or supersede Galili’s LHHM/FEA comparison; it converts prior biomechanical and clinical information into an explicit, inspectable Layer-1 planning coordinate system, continuous candidate sweep, and constraint-aware ranking framework.

1. Papers to imitate for structure and tone

Galili, White Zeira & Marom, 2022, Royal Society Open Science — “Numerical biomechanics modelling of indirect mitral annuloplasty treatments for functional mitral regurgitation.”

Why it matters: this is the immediate computational predecessor. It explicitly compared generic IMA-CS and IMA-AP treatments using the Living Heart Human Model, finite-element analysis, and subsequent leakage modeling. Its discrete intervention levels included IMA-CS 14/18/22% shortening and IMA-AP 30/50/70% shortening. It also reported a non-monotonic IMA-AP result: the 50% case had lower ROA than further shortening to 70%. 
PubMed
+1

Imitate: the clean progression mechanism → intervention parameterization → geometry → functional surrogate → comparison; the explicit side-by-side treatment taxonomy; the willingness to discuss non-monotonic behavior rather than forcing “more cinch is better.”

Do not copy: its claims to FEA, beating-heart modeling, LHHM mechanics, leaflet contact, ROA calculation, or SPH leakage. Those belong to Galili’s model, not this Layer-1 surrogate.

Most important novelty implication: never claim the first computational comparison of IMA-CS and IMA-AP. Galili already did that.

Also, do not write “Galili showed that 50% suture = 0% AP.” Galili did not. In your manuscript, that must be identified as your planner coordinate convention/reference transformation.

Worthley et al., 2015, JACC: Cardiovascular Interventions — first-in-human MAVERIC/ARTO study.

Why it matters: it provides the strongest direct clinical bridge for an AP-directed CS-to-septal concept. In the initial 11-patient study, the annular AP diameter went from 45.0 to 38.7 mm at 30 days, described by the authors as a 14% reduction. 
PubMed

Do not copy: efficacy language into the surrogate manuscript. Do not say that your 18% modeled AP result predicts ARTO efficacy. Do not use MAVERIC's clinical MR volume as a calibration for your physics-regurgitation percentage.

Best role in your paper: establish that an AP-directed transcatheter annuloplasty can produce an AP change on the order reported clinically, so the planner can identify whether a modeled candidate lies inside, near, or beyond a clinically observed geometric range.

Patterson et al., MAVERIC one-year and two-year reports.

The larger MAVERIC cohort reported AP diameter changing from 41.4 mm at baseline to 36.0 mm at 30 days—a 13% decrease—and 35.3 mm at one year. The two-year report described persistence of the AP reduction.

Do not copy: longitudinal clinical inference. Your Layer-1 calculation has no follow-up, remodeling, outcome, durability, or survival dimension.

For your manuscript, “approximately 13–15% clinically observed AP reduction in MAVERIC reports” is more defensible than treating exactly 14% or 15% as a universal target.

Witte et al., 2019, REDUCE-FMR, JACC: Heart Failure.

Why it matters: REDUCE-FMR was a randomized, sham-controlled Carillon study designed around MR and LV remodeling, with coronary angiography used procedurally to assess circumflex compromise. 
JACC

Imitate: restrained causal language, endpoint hierarchy, and explicit device-versus-control framing. It is a good stylistic counterweight to overconfident computational prose.

Do not copy: statements such as “the model reproduces the REDUCE-FMR treatment effect.” It does not. REDUCE-FMR can support directionality and clinical context for CS-based annuloplasty, not numerical validation of the Python equations.

Recommended wording: “directionally consistent with clinical observations of CS-based annuloplasty,” not “validated against REDUCE-FMR.”

Rottländer et al., 2021, Catheterization and Cardiovascular Interventions — CT planning for CS-based annuloplasty.

This is the key source for your CS–LCx constraint. In 25 Carillon patients, the authors found that a CS-to-Cx distance <8.6 mm specifically in the distal device landing zone predicted circumflex compromise in their ROC analysis. They did not establish ≥8.6 mm as a universal guarantee of safety. 
PubMed

Imitate: the procedural-planning vocabulary: candidate eligibility, anatomical screening, landing-zone-specific constraint, feasible versus infeasible configuration.

Do not copy: turn the ROC threshold into a deterministic biomechanical law.

Therefore C3 should preferably say: “a literature-informed CS–LCx screening constraint based on the reported <8.6-mm risk threshold,” rather than simply “CS–LCx ≥8.6 mm is safe.”

de Oliveira et al., 2023, Medical Engineering & Physics — “A geometry-based finite element tool for evaluating mitral valve biomechanics.”

This paper is useful mainly for manuscript architecture. It presents a parameterized computational tool, clearly states what the framework generates, evaluates different geometries, and distinguishes model flexibility from biological truth. 
PubMed

Imitate: modular Methods; explicit inputs and outputs; reproducible parameter definitions; “what this tool is for” language; separate validation/verification/illustrative-use concepts.

Do not copy: FE terminology such as leaflet stresses, chordal forces, contact mechanics, closure dynamics, material-property validation, or biomechanical validation.

Your analogous tone is: “rapid parametric screening tool,” “candidate-ranking framework,” or “pre-high-fidelity planning layer.”

Siefert et al., 2015, Computer Methods in Biomechanics and Biomedical Engineering — “Isolated effect of geometry on mitral valve function for in silico model development.”

The paper deliberately perturbs individual geometric factors and separately tracks coaptation, MR, jet direction, and strain. It is particularly relevant because it demonstrates the value of controlled parameter perturbations rather than collapsing everything into one composite endpoint.

Do not copy: its “ground-truth” language; Siefert had physical in-vitro measurements. Your YAML/physics surrogate is not ground truth.

Open Heart

Do not copy: “passed fatigue,” “fatigue safe,” “infinite life,” or FDA-equivalent device qualification. A Python Layer-1 strain estimator cannot make those claims.

Prefer: “alternating-strain screening criterion <0.4%, adopted as a conservative engineering planning constraint.”

Even better: call it a screen, not a validation threshold.

2. Recommended full-paper writing framework
Title

Avoid titles containing “finite element,” “biomechanical simulation of the human heart,” or “patient-specific prediction.”

A suitable structural pattern is:

A constraint-aware Layer-1 computational planner for indirect mitral annuloplasty in functional mitral regurgitation

Clinically referenced dose mapping and constrained parametric planning of indirect mitral annuloplasty

A rapid surrogate framework for dose harmonization and preoperative screening of indirect mitral annuloplasty strategies

The word surrogate should appear either in the title, abstract, or first Methods paragraph.

Abstract

Use five logical moves.

Background: IMA-CS and AP-directed IMA act through different geometric mechanisms; prior high-fidelity work has already compared them.

Gap: discrete device-shortening percentages are difficult to translate into a common clinically interpretable planning coordinate, and candidate selection must also respect anatomical/device constraints.

Methods: introduce the Python Layer-1 surrogate, dose-coordinate mapping, continuous sweep, constraints, planner objective, sensitivity analysis.

Results: give the seed-42 result exactly and narrowly:

dual IMA-AP 60%

modeled AP reduction 18%

physics-regurgitation metric 0.152%

central jet

Conclusion: candidate screening/hypothesis prioritization before higher-fidelity modeling or procedural evaluation.

The abstract should explicitly state that the 0.152% is a surrogate physics metric and not clinical regurgitant volume or regurgitant fraction.

Paragraph 1 — Clinical/mechanistic problem

FMR arises from ventricular/annular geometric dysfunction.

Introduce indirect annuloplasty.

Distinguish:

IMA-CS: CS-based annular reshaping.

IMA-AP: CS-to-septal/AP-directed shortening.

Keep this clinically descriptive rather than computational.

Paragraph 2 — Existing evidence

Progress from clinical to computational:

Carillon / REDUCE-FMR establishes clinical CS-based context. 
JACC

ARTO / MAVERIC establishes clinically observed AP-directed reshaping, approximately 13–14% at early follow-up in published reports.

Galili already compared IMA-CS and IMA-AP computationally with LHHM/FEA and discrete treatment settings. 
PubMed

This paragraph prevents the novelty problem before a reviewer raises it.

Paragraph 3 — Actual gap

This should carry most of the novelty argument:

Prior work establishes mechanical and clinical plausibility, but it does not by itself provide a lightweight planning layer that places heterogeneous intervention controls on a common AP-oriented dose coordinate, continuously interrogates the candidate space, and rejects configurations that violate predefined anatomical or engineering constraints.

That is much safer than saying nobody has compared the two therapies.

Paragraph 4 — Contributions

State C1–C3 and optional C4 explicitly.

I would modify the labels slightly.

C1 — Clinically referenced dose-coordinate harmonization.

Translate intervention/suture control into a common planner AP coordinate.

Explicitly define the planner convention in which Galili 50% suture shortening is assigned the reference value of 0% planner AP cinch.

Crucially: state that this is a normalization/mapping convention, not a result reported by Galili and not an experimentally established clinical conversion.

C2 — Continuous constrained candidate exploration.

Replace a small set of discrete cases with a continuous or finely sampled sweep.

Apply the same scoring structure throughout.

Produce a preoperative candidate ranking rather than a retrospective comparison of isolated cases.

C3 — Anatomical and device-integrity screening.

CS–LCx screening based on the literature-reported <8.6-mm distal-zone risk threshold. 
PubMed

NiTi alternating-strain screen <0.4%.

Make both admissibility gates, not terms whose violation can be compensated by better predicted MR.

Optional C4 — Matched-dose architectural comparison.

Compare dual versus single AP sutures at matched modeled AP reduction.

This is more novel and cleaner than saying “dual versus single at nominal suture percentage,” because matched AP% attempts to isolate architecture from dose.

This subsection is mandatory.

State immediately:

Python Layer-1 surrogate.

No new LHHM computation.

No Abaqus simulation.

No FSI.

No leaflet/tissue stress calculation unless actually present.

No patient-specific procedural prediction.

Purpose: fast candidate screening before high-fidelity analysis.

A useful sentence is:

The framework is intended to rank and exclude candidate configurations under transparent assumptions rather than reproduce full mitral-device biomechanics.

7. Likely rejection risks

Risk 1: novelty collapses to “Galili, but simpler.”
This is the largest risk. Galili already performed the high-fidelity IMA-CS versus IMA-AP comparison. 
PubMed
 Your paper therefore needs to demonstrate that the planning formulation itself produces information absent from the original discrete FEA study: common dose coordinate, continuous sweep, hard constraints, sensitivity, and candidate ranking.

Risk 2: C1 looks arbitrary.
A reviewer may immediately ask why Galili 50% suture shortening is assigned zero AP cinch, particularly because Galili's actual 50% simulation produced substantial anatomical change. 
researchgate.net
 Do not hide this transformation. Define its mathematical purpose and label it a reference-coordinate convention. If it is partly empirically motivated, document precisely which observations determine the mapping.

Risk 3: “clinical dose mapping” overclaims calibration.
MAVERIC provides clinical AP measurements, but that does not automatically create a validated conversion from Galili suture shortening to clinical AP reduction. 
JACC
+1
 Unless there is formal calibration, “clinically referenced mapping” is safer than “clinical dose conversion.”

Risk 4: the 8.6-mm inequality is reversed rhetorically.
The source found <8.6 mm predictive of compromise in a particular distal landing zone; it did not prove that all configurations at ≥8.6 mm are safe. 
PubMed
 Reviewers in structural cardiology will notice this distinction.

Risk 5: the NiTi threshold is treated as device qualification.
The cited 0.4% value derives from specific nitinol fatigue testing reported in prior stent literature and should not be generalized into a universal pass/fail law. 
ScienceDirect
 Your code can enforce <0.4% as a conservative screen, but the paper must say what it is—and what it is not.

Risk 6: the surrogate MR percentage is mistaken for a clinical quantity.
This needs redundant safeguards: variable name, Methods definition, Results caption, and Discussion limitation. Do not put “MR volume (%)” on an axis if it is not MR volume.

Risk 7: YAML overwhelms physics.
If ranking cannot be decomposed into physics-derived versus configured terms, reviewers may conclude that the “planner” simply encodes the desired answer. Report component scores and at least one physics-only or ablation analysis.

Risk 8: η sensitivity undermines an overly categorical conclusion.
Because ±20% can move the recommendation among dual AP 60%, dual AP 70%, and CS 20%, the scientifically correct conclusion is that the nominal optimum is assumption-sensitive. That is publishable if framed as an uncertainty result; it is damaging only if the manuscript simultaneously calls dual AP 60% a robust optimum.

Risk 9: one seed is presented as general validation.
Seed 42 should be your reproducible worked case. If feasible, add a compact multi-seed or parameter-perturbation stability analysis. If not, keep claims explicitly scenario-specific.

Risk 10: C4 is confounded by dose.
Dual versus single is useful only if you compare at matched or closely matched modeled AP reduction. Otherwise a reviewer cannot tell whether jet differences arise from architecture or simply a different effective cinch.

The strongest paper is therefore not “a new simulator of IMA.” It is a transparent pre-high-fidelity decision scaffold that solves the translation problem between heterogeneous intervention parameters, geometric dose, anatomical/device constraints, and candidate ranking while exposing sensitivity to assumptions. That positioning is both more novel relative to Galili and more defensible for the actual Python Layer-1 implementation.
